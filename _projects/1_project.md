Microbial safety in vegetables is a growing concern due to frequent reports of foodborne illnesses linked to pathogen exposure, necessitating improved safety management across the entire supply chain. Climate change further complicates these efforts, as altered temperatures, precipitation patterns, and extreme weather events can accelerate the proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms. This proposal aims to develop predictive models and risk assessments for pathogen infections associated with locally produced leafy greens (spinach, water spinach, cabbage, and mustard greens) in Central Java, Indonesia, accounting for both local weather variations and global climate change. The study will first utilize Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) to detect key foodborne pathogens—*Salmonella enterica*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, and *Staphylococcus aureus*—in vegetable samples. Subsequently, predictive models incorporating temperature, humidity, and precipitation data will forecast potential contamination levels and evaluate pathogen surface-to-vegetable transfer ratios. Finally, the research will assess the health consequences of consuming contaminated vegetables to determine the overall impact of climate change on infection risks. Ultimately, this study will enhance our understanding of climate-driven impacts on microbial food safety and establish a foundation for effective future mitigation strategies.
