### The Quick Summary

This study looks at egg farms in Saint Kitts, a small island. Before this, no one had really studied how these farms keep their chickens safe from sickness or what kinds of diseases the chickens might have. To find out, the researchers visited 11 commercial farms. They asked the farmers questions about how they keep their farms clean and safe, which is called biosecurity. They also took blood and throat samples from the chickens to check for common bird sicknesses. The main goal was to understand how well farms protect their chickens and which diseases are present. This information is important for making sure eggs are safe to eat and helping farms keep their chickens healthy.

### Practical Implications

This study provides crucial baseline data on poultry biosecurity practices and disease prevalence within Saint Kitts' commercial poultry sector. For food science, understanding these factors is paramount because inadequate biosecurity and widespread diseases directly impact the safety and quality of poultry products, especially eggs, for human consumption. This research allows food scientists to identify specific risks, such as potential zoonotic pathogens, and develop targeted interventions. These interventions could include improved biosecurity protocols, vaccine strategies, or enhanced diagnostic tools to mitigate disease spread. Ultimately, this leads to stronger food safety standards, better animal welfare, and a more sustainable and reliable local food supply chain for poultry products.

### Potential Use in Indonesia

In Indonesia, similar studies focusing on biosecurity and disease prevalence in local poultry farms are vital, particularly for the vast informal food sector. Findings could inform strategies to improve food safety in traditional markets and among street food vendors, where poultry and eggs are widely sold. This is especially relevant in tropical climates where disease transmission can be rapid, helping to protect consumers from foodborne illnesses and stabilize local supply chains from potential disease outbreaks.
